1. Cyber warfare and Cambridge Analytica

Can the Cambridge Analytica situation be described as cyber warfare? Is every hack part of cyber war?

Usually no for Analytica, and no for ordinary hacking.

  • Cambridge Analytica harvested Facebook profile data and used it for political targeting. That is influence, profiling and unlawful processing, not an armed attack on infrastructure.
  • Cyber war language is tighter: typically a state (or its proxy), a threshold of harm, and a link to armed conflict or the law of armed conflict. The Tallinn Manual is the usual reference.
  • Lecture 1 groups Analytica with Facebook election manipulation under a later "triality" of privacy risk: harm can hit the person, the organisation, and society (elections, trust).
  • Guardian: Cambridge Analytica files
  • ICO investigation (UK)
  • CCDCOE: Tallinn Manual
  • ICRC on cyber warfare

5. ENISA PIA impact levels

Four levels aimed at harm to the individual (SME guideline, ch. 3):

  • Low - minor inconvenience (re-enter data, irritation).
  • Medium - significant inconvenience they can still overcome (extra cost, stress, denied service).
  • High - serious difficulties (lost job, money taken, health worse, blacklisting).
  • Very high - serious or irreversible (cannot work, long-term illness, death).
  • ENISA: Guidelines for SMEs on the security of personal data processing (2016)

Notebook "basic principles"

The page lists only three: security, accountability, transparency. That is a first-day subset. Full Art. 5 also has lawfulness, fairness, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy and storage limitation. Integrity and confidentiality is the security principle. Accountability is Art. 5(2): you must be able to show compliance.

If data are lost, is there a time limit?

Yes, but two clocks:

  • Art. 33 - tell the supervisory authority without undue delay, and where feasible within 72 hours.
  • Art. 34 - tell the people if the breach is likely to mean a high risk to their rights. No 72-hour number here. The duty is without undue delay.

Tracker: search Norway on enforcementtracker.com.

Week 2 Part 2 - The Toulmin model

From the flipped video The model (slides sent 23 Aug 2026). Qualifier now has its own slide. Backing and rebuttal still appear only in the smoking diagrams.

Signs of a good argument

  • Persuasive: it is effective in getting support for a claim.
  • Uses deliberative rhetoric: focusing on the future and what to do next. Not judicial or epideictic.
  • Relevant: relates to the question that is debated.

The Toulmin model

Developed by Stephen Toulmin, an English philosopher interested in what makes everyday arguments successful. The model was based on reviewing court cases.

It is now widely used to construct and de-construct arguments: debate, research, essays, dinner tables and cocktail parties.

Its prime function is not to describe the order of the argument, but that the right components are in place to make it persuasive.

Example: you want your colleague to stop smoking (slide 17, Kyrre Begnum)

  • Claim: You should stop smoking.
  • Grounds: Smoking leads to severe health risk for yourself. Smoking can affect the people around you.
  • Warrant (explicit): You cannot disregard the advice of the medical community.
  • Warrant (implicit): You should take health advice from your friends seriously.
  • Backing: We know what happens to people who ignore unanimous advice from doctors.
  • Qualifier: At least try to reduce it as much as possible and set a goal to stop before a date.
  • Rebuttal: I know it is difficult, but there are other products that can help you control the nicotine addiction.

Example: you want your spouse to stop smoking

Same claim and almost the same map. The lecturer contrasts colleague vs spouse to show that the implicit warrant changes with the audience (the warrant-in-context point).

  • Claim: You should stop smoking.
  • Grounds: Smoking leads to severe health risks for yourself. Smoking can affect people around you.
  • Warrant (explicit): You cannot disregard the advice of the medical community.
  • Warrant (implicit): You should respect the concerns and advice from those who love you. (Colleague version: take health advice from your friends seriously.)
  • Backing: We know what happens to people who ignore unanimous advice from doctors.
  • Qualifier: At least try to reduce it as much as possible and set a goal to stop before a date.
  • Rebuttal: I know it is difficult, but there are other products that can help you control the nicotine addiction.

Claim

What you want your audience to accept: opinion, action, vote, support and so on. Can be for or against a proposal.

In some cases you may not see a claim even though the rest of the Toulmin model is there, especially in scientific and technical presentations.

Grounds

  • Can be evidence, data, statistics, anecdotes.
  • Should present the reasoning behind the claim.
  • May also contain evidence of expertise or authority.
  • Most likely the backbone of the argument, but may affect people differently based on factors like their own emotional investment.
  • In research, the results and their analysis provide the core of your grounds.

Warrant

Simply put: Why you should listen to me.

  • The warrant can itself be a longer argument.
  • Warrants can be data and evidence, or values.
  • The warrant helps put the argument in a context. It can help explain why the same claim and grounds work differently in different settings.

Qualifiers (slide 22)

  • An unqualified claim would have to be defended for all extreme cases.
  • Qualifiers can adjust the guarantee of success: most likely, greatest chance, 90% of ...
  • Qualifiers can also provide insurance against insufficient grounds: considering all other options, after careful deliberation, given what we know right now.

Listening as actual listening (slide 23)

  • Debaters spend their time keeping track of the debate, instead of thinking about what they want to say next.
  • The Toulmin model can be used as a structure by which to categorize what you hear (and do not hear).
  • A technique called flow lets them quickly note what is being said in a system that makes line-by-line refutation possible.
  • Flows are created through the use of symbols that signify meta-information of the arguments.

Week 2 Part 3 - The affirmative

From the flipped video toulmin_part_3 (34:46, Kyrre Begnum). Case slides now include the composition notice and a Part 4 teaser. About 33:44 of 34:46. The self-test is not done yet.

The affirmative (Roosevelt, man in the arena)

The slide quotes Roosevelt: it is not the critic who counts. Credit belongs to the man who is actually in the arena.

Overlay: Arguing for the affirmative.

Use as: the affirmative takes the risk of proposing change. Critics who only point out stumbles do not get the credit.

Arguing for the affirmative (slide 26)

The affirmative argues for change by offering a proposition.

One needs to cover three aspects to be persuasive:

  • Indict the status quo: What is wrong and what harm does it do?
  • Demonstrate solvency: Does your proposition actually address the problem and reduce harm?
  • Be an example of the proposition: Don't replace evil with evil.

Three factors (slide 27, expanded)

Building a good argument comes from an understanding of three factors:

  • The audience: Who do you want to convince? What are their values? What is their situation or context?
  • Prior research: Enough relevant data and grounds. Also needed in anticipation of counter arguments. (Background chapter!)
  • and Nuance: Use of qualifiers. Correct agency.

The importance of agency 1 (slide 28)

  • The agents in an argument are those who should actually DO what you propose, and may not be the same as or all of your audience.
  • Having the wrong agents in a proposal may reduce the solvency of the harm. ... and Mexico will pay for it!
  • The agents themselves may not feel enabled or empowered to enact on the proposal.
  • Unclear agency may also lead to distracting discussions and confusion, which nuance and qualifiers can help avoid.

Qualified / nuanced version of the same claim:

... and Mexico will indirectly pay for it through a negotiation of our agreements which would indirectly create a financial surplus sufficient to cover the cost!

Arguing from the perspective of the agent

  • A persuasive argument must highlight how the controversy, harm and cost affects the agents themselves.
  • An often overlooked factor is the opportunity cost of a proposal, as committing to an action may limit the ability to respond to future opportunities.

Where to find arguments in scientific papers and thesis (slide 30)

The introduction's claim: Addressing the problem I'm describing is important and worth spending resources on.

  • Heavy on indictment and harm. The starting point of the introduction with a narrowing from domain down to the specific issue.
  • Must demonstrate solvency. The proposed impact usually right before the problem statement.
  • Low on backing and warrant. That's for the background. Implicit warrant: the problem's nature warrants a scientific approach.
  • Should have nuance. The operationalization after the problem statement.

Where to find arguments: the approach (slide 31)

The approach's claim: My plan is feasible and presents an adequate scientific approach to answer the problem.

  • Grounds, warrant and backing: the research method and its tradition; the amount of work relative to the timeframe.
  • Must demonstrate solvency: the data gathered speaks to what we need to uncover; the experiments cover the relevant aspects of interest; the plan is detailed enough to demonstrate control.

Example: using Toulmin when preparing a presentation (slide 32)

Case: your faculty will hold a workshop for teachers on using flipped classroom teaching approaches. You are invited to give an introduction in order to motivate the attendees.

Claim ??? (slide 33)

Draft line on the slide: We should adopt flipped classroom approaches to teaching...?

The claim is still being formulated. The question marks are part of the point: you do not start with a finished claim.

Indictment examples (slides around 19:29 and slide 34)

  • Students are digitized and require a high degree of flexibility.
  • We don't have sufficient opportunity to engage directly with students when we are most needed.
  • Students expect us to deliver teaching that is most beneficial to them.

Highlighted on the slide: Indictment of the status quo!

Identifying harms (slide 35)

  • Students are not getting the best education.
  • We are not fulfilling our obligation to produce the best outcomes from our tax-based financing.
  • We are unable to work in ways that are more motivating and rewarding.

Grounds, including solvency of the harms (slide 36)

  • Practical information: What it takes to transition from a traditional to a flipped classroom approach.
  • Our values: Making a positive impact on students is profound and meaningful.
  • Anecdotal evidence: My own experience.
  • Scientific evidence: Published studies.
  • New incentives: Becoming a merited educator will lead to a pay raise and status.

These grounds also speak to solvency (how the proposal addresses the harms). They mix anecdotes and published studies.

Warrant (slide 37)

  • Explicit: The studies I have presented are similar to our teaching environment and their results are believable.
  • Implicit: We should aspire to continuously adjust our practices to those which currently provide the best outcomes.
  • Backing: A colleague of mine has done the same and it also provided good results there.

Qualifiers and agency (slide 38)

  • Only those who are motivated will prioritize this.
  • Some want to work on a different aspect of their career.
  • Some feel structurally bound by the system.

The agents: Teachers who are inspired by engaging teaching methods and want to advance their career.

Rebuttals (slide 39)

  • Objection: We don't have the skills. Rebuttal: People who teach, usually know how to learn. It's not about what you know right now.
  • Objection: There's no real incentive. Rebuttal: No longer true.
  • Objection: It's a lot of work. Rebuttal: Yes, but only initially. You'll end up with more flexibility in the long run and can use that on other career aspects.

Claim before vs after (slide 40)

  • Before: We should adopt flipped classroom approaches to teaching...?
  • After: Moving towards a flipped classroom approach is a good way to teach in a meaningful and engaging way while advancing your career.

The after-claim is already qualified (moving towards), tied to values (meaningful and engaging) and to agency / career (advancing your career).

Notice: The Toulmin model does not prescribe an order or structure of the communication itself. Building a presentation from all pieces is now a matter of composition and rhetoric.

Week 2 Part 4 - The negative

From the flipped video toulmin_part_4 (22:01, Kyrre Begnum). Video slides now include the closing and a Part 5 teaser. The Canvas self-test is not marked done until you submit.

The critic (Churchill, 1939)

The slide quotes Churchill (1939):

Criticism may not be agreeable, but it is necessary. It fulfills the same function as pain in the human body; it calls attention to the development of an unhealthy state of things. If it is heeded in time, danger may be averted; if it is suppressed, a fatal distemper may develop.

Arguing against the affirmative (slide 43)

  • Arguing for the negative is often an argument against a proposal made by others.
  • The negative can attack from several perspectives, but can risk being motivated by the wrong values, i.e. a personal victory instead of a good debate.
  • A good case for the negative has three components:
    • Direct disadvantages of the proposal
    • Counter proposals
    • Critique of assumptions

Direct disadvantages 1: Inherency of the proposal (slide 44)

  • Attacks on the inherency of a proposal means pointing out obstacles, previous attempts or even better current efforts. We're doing this already. There's already a better fix.
  • Not limited to feasibility, and can be attitudinal. Things are fine right now.
  • You're not disagreeing with the problem or grounds, but point out other inherent factors that weaken the proposal.
  • Uncovering inherency issues requires research into the proposal and the problem within the status quo.

Direct disadvantages 2: Attacking the harms (slide 45)

  • The core of most affirmative proposals is that the status quo is doing a harm. Underlining the magnitude of the harm can be a powerful tool, but may also be tested by the negative. Are we really sure all the worst case harm is realistic? It won't be as bad.
  • In some cases, the proposed harm can become a positive in the end. Yes, people loose their jobs, but if we offer them a new career, they would be able to earn more and pay more tax. (Slide spelling: loose. Meaning: lose jobs.)
  • One can risk being too cavalier if not combined with other arguments.

Direct disadvantages 3: Solvency of the proposal (slide 46)

  • Attacking on the solvency of the proposal means arguing that the proposal will not solve the described problem.
    • It won't play out as you say it will.
    • This will do little to address the core issue.
    • You're fighting the symptom, not the disease.
  • Not the same as attacking the feasibility of the proposition.
  • Attacks of the solvency will strengthen all other negative attacks, i.e. the possible harm of the proposal outweigh the weak prospect of positives.

Counter proposals (slide 47)

  • Offer a variance of the proposal or a completely different proposal.
  • Does not need the same scrutiny, but if well prepared can be a devastating blow to the affirmative.
  • Counter proposals should always be deliberative, and not interrogative.
  • Don't: Why did you not consider shorting the stocks instead?
  • Instead: I think shorting the stocks is a better choice, because ....

Attacking the assumptions (slide 48)

  • Assumptions can be found in the warrants and grounds: You assume a 50-year old study is relevant today
  • Assumptions also exist in the indictment of the status quo: You say unemployment is bad, but unemployment in one sector is a necessary and natural re-adjustment of the markets.
  • One can also "flip the warrant" combined with a counter proposal: If we really want students to succeed, then we should have stricter admission requirements and not pretend everyone can get what they want! Do you think they're debating flipped classroom in law school? They don't, because they only have students who really want to be there and accept that it is hard. In fact, they are proud of their achievement in the end for that reason!

Closing: why bother?

The slide asks: Wow, sounds like arguing for any proposal is almost impossible. Why bother?

  • Arguing for the affirmative SHOULD be hard!
  • Every proposal brings change and change adds risk. NOTE: Canvas Part 4 self-test Q2 does NOT accept that option even though it is the slide wording.
  • However, arguing against a proposal should not be confused with being against change. Change may be inevitable, in which case debate could help determine the best proposals. This is why a point of stasis is so important.

Week 2 Part 5 - Making decisions based on debate

From the flipped video toulmin_part_5 (25:12, Kyrre Begnum). Early slides only (51-52, When voting doesn't work, 54-55). Part 5 is not marked done.

Let's refocus on the goal: making good decisions (slide 51)

  • Leaders and managers have several options to make informed decisions.
  • Whenever they want input or advice, they would have informal conversations with trusted employees, organize a meeting / workshop or hearing.
  • How would what we have covered until now assist in reaching the goal of good decisions?

When pros vs cons don't work (slide 52)

The participants brainstorm pros and cons which are written on a whiteboard.

Problems:

  • Favors strong and verbal personalities
  • Has no nuance
  • Is not always an aggregate
  • No visible value or weight of each point made, and assumes it is the number that counts

When voting doesn't work

Employees are asked to propose actions, which are written on a board. Everyone votes on them.

Problems:

  • Favors those who can handle impromptu elections and get the most airtime for their proposal.
  • Gives little time to thoroughly prepare proposals, so the old and faithful proposals take center stage.
  • If you propose it, you have to defend it, adding risk to looking foolish.
  • Sometimes, the best proposal is not the one with the most votes.

When a free discussion doesn't work (slide 54)

The topic is introduced in a meeting and everyone is asked for their opinion.

Problems:

  • Dominant personalities prevail.
  • No one has adequate time to prepare, so old battle-lines are redrawn.
  • If you propose it, you have to defend it, adding risk to looking foolish.

What leaders can do to promote valuable input (slide 55)

  • Predefine the propositions and point of stasis.
  • Specify the values by which a decision will be made at the end.
  • Provide enough time to prepare good arguments.
  • Assign designated debaters for propositions.
  • Write RDF (Reason For Decision) so employees can understand the reasoning of the leader.

18. Information security vs cyber security

Notebook arrow: information security points to access control, plus misuse, loss and so on. That matches Peltier in Lecture 1: keep data used properly, and stop unauthorised change, destruction, disclosure, loss or access. Paper files count. Legal barriers count.

Next line: cyber security involves all info on your computer. Official wording is tighter: cyber security is the subset for information stored on computers or sent between computers. Information security is the umbrella.

19. Hierarchy of trust

Each layer trusts the layer below it. A higher layer, a user, an admin, or crypto has to impose any other trust.

  1. Application

20. TASK page: names and frameworks, not a build list

The page is headed TASK and mixes defender words (scan) with attacker examples (virus, rootkit, bot, botnet, DDoS). Treat it as a map of Lecture 1 terms. Do not write or run a virus, rootkit, bot or DDoS. The course wants you to name the class, place it on NIST / Kill Chain / ATT&CK, and know how a defender would detect or stop it.

What the lines mean

  • Scan for virus, trojan and so on - defender side. NIST Detect. Virus / worm / Trojan are how malware spreads. Spyware, ransomware, bot and rootkit are intent classes. The same sample can sit on both axes.
  • Rootkit / hide - intent class: hide malware, often with admin rights, so normal tools may look clean. Exam point: hard to detect, not harmless.
  • Bot / botnet / DDoS - a bot is remote control; a botnet is many bots; DDoS hits availability by flooding from many machines. C2 is Kill Chain stage 6. Blocking C2 can stop the attack after install.
  • Build on NIST - CSF 2.0 six functions: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover, Govern.
  • Use MITRE ATT&CK - finer kill-chain knowledge base (tactics, techniques, mitigations, detection). Site: attack.mitre.org.
  • Lockheed Kill Chain - seven stages: Reconnaissance, Weaponization, Delivery, Exploitation, Installation, Command and control, Action on objectives. The page only wrote 1 and 2 plus "and so on".
  • Example: cafe Wi-Fi, then work Wi-Fi - a trust-boundary story. An untrusted network, then a trusted one. Place it as recon / delivery risk, or as a user who carries a dirty laptop across the hierarchy of trust. Not a how-to.
