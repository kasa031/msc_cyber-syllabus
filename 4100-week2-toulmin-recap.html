In-class sheet first. Recap of Parts 1-5 below. P32 N020.025, Mon 24 Aug.

Stasis · Proposition · Opposition · Judge · P1 · P2 · P3 · P4 · P5

Proposition

"Technology is the source of good in the world."

Point of stasis (shared first)

If the room has no shared stasis, people talk past each other. Do not start slides or speeches until this is agreed.

  • One question only: Should we accept that technology is the source of good in the world?
  • Not: is tech useful, is tech evil, who likes gadgets, or who is a better speaker.
  • Disassociate the argument from the person. Best decision, not winning.

Proposition (affirmative)

  • Indict status quo: without treating tech as a source of good, we leave preventable harms in place (disease, isolation, unsafe water, slow knowledge).
  • Solvency: tech must solve that harm, not just be feasible to build. Vaccines and diagnostics cut death. Networks move knowledge. That is solvency. "We can ship more apps" is only feasibility.
  • Be an example: argue as if this room will act; show one clean case, not a catalogue.
  • Qualifier: "the source" is extreme. Say a primary source, often, most likely. Leave a rebuttal: tech can harm if agency is missing.
  • Agency: who must do it (designers, firms, states), not always this audience. Watch opportunity cost.

Say (affirmative)

Claim: we should treat technology as a primary source of good, because it uniquely scales solutions to shared harms.

Grounds: vaccines, sanitation systems, and research networks reduced death and isolation at a scale no single person can match.

Warrant: if a means systematically solves the harm we indict, it is a source of good, not only a gadget.

Qualifier + agency: not every tool, and not by itself. People and institutions must deploy it; otherwise the status quo harm stays.

Opposition (negative)

  • Direct disadvantages: inherency (the harm is already inside tech: bias, lock-in, weapons); attack harms (those deaths are not mainly from "too little tech"); attack solvency (tech does not solve the named harm, or it trades one harm for another). Feasibility is not the target.
  • Counter proposals (deliberative, not questions): "We should treat human judgement and institutions as the source of good, and technology as a tool." Not "but what about AI risk?"
  • Critique / flip the warrant: the aff warrant is "what enables good is the source." Flip: a tool is not a source; agents are. Smoking-style: same grounds, new implicit warrant.

Say (opposition)

The status quo harm is not lack of tech. It is unaccountable tech. So the indictment misfires.

Even if vaccines work, that does not prove tech is the source of good. People, ethics, and law decided to use them. Solvency fails on the word "source."

Counter proposal: fund and constrain technology as a means. Locate the source of good in responsible agents, not in artefacts.

Judge (what you can say)

  • Aim: the best decision, not who won the room.
  • Hold the stasis. If speakers leave it, pull them back.
  • Attack the argument, not the person. Both sides are critics.
  • Weight, do not count, the points. Flow / line-by-line if you can.
  • End with an RFD (Reason For Decision).

Say (judge)

Before we start: our stasis is whether technology is the source of good in the world. Stay on that.

I am not scoring who sounded confident. I am deciding which claim is better warranted.

Affirmative, I heard feasibility. I need solvency: does tech actually fix the harm you indicted?

Opposition, a question is not a counter proposal. Tell me what we should do instead.

I weigh the flipped warrant on "source" more than the list of gadgets. One strong clash beats five weak points.

RFD: I decide for [side] because they kept stasis and showed [solvency / failed source-claim / better agency], not because they were louder.

Part 1 - Why debates are important

  • Prerequisites: a point of stasis; the argument is disassociated from the debater; the aim is the best decision, not winning.
  • Fill-in: a clearly defined stasis (not "argument").
  • Three payoffs: vantage point awareness, communication skills, cultural reinforcement.
  • Meeting problem: no point of stasis, so people talk past each other.

Part 2 - The model

  • A good argument is persuasive, deliberative rhetoric (future), and relevant.
  • Stephen Toulmin, from court cases: get the right components, not a fixed order.
  • Six parts: Claim, Grounds, Warrant, Backing, Qualifier, Rebuttal.
  • Claim = what you want believed. Grounds = support (anecdotes are OK; not only "provable" evidence). Warrant = "why listen".
  • Smoking examples: colleague vs spouse changes the implicit warrant.
  • Qualifiers for extreme cases: most likely, 90%, insurance-style phrases.
  • In debate: flow / line-by-line refutation.
  • Self-test traps: deliberative, not "general"; match the table; False that grounds must be only provable evidence.

Part 3 - Affirmative

  • Three aspects: indict the status quo; demonstrate solvency (solves the harm, not the same as feasible); be an example.
  • Know the audience; do prior research (background chapter); keep nuance (qualifiers + agency).
  • Agency = who must do it, not always the audience. Watch opportunity cost.
  • Papers: intro claim vs approach claim.
  • Flipped case: indictment, harms, grounds + solvency, warrant/backing, qualifiers, rebuttals, then a refined claim.
  • Toulmin does not prescribe delivery order.

Part 4 - Negative

  • Three routes: direct disadvantages, counter proposals, critique of assumptions.
  • Also: inherency; attack harms; attack solvency (not feasibility).
  • Counter proposals are deliberative, not interrogative.
  • Flip the warrant.
  • The affirmative should be hard to beat.
  • Canvas rejected "change adds risk" as the Q2 key (score 1/2, no retake).

Part 5 - Decisions

  • Pros/cons lists, voting, and free discussion fail as decision tools.
  • Leaders: predefine the issue + stasis; surface values; set time; designate debaters; give an RFD/RDF (Reason For Decision).
  • Culture: scientists are humans. Informal settings: discuss, do not attack.
  • Do not dismiss dissenters. Toulmin: to condemn dissenters as dissidents is to condemn yourselves.
